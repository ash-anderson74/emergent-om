# Pattern: Behaviour to Constraint Mapping

## Pattern Type

**Diagnostic Pattern**

## Intent

To use recurring behaviour as evidence about the system, identifying the constraints and structural conditions that make that behaviour rational before attempting to change it.

The purpose is not to correct people. It is to understand what the system is causing, rewarding, protecting, or making necessary.

***

{% hint style="info" %}
**Lineage note**

Behaviour → Constraint Mapping combines the Local Rationality principle from safety science with systems-thinking inquiry into constraints, feedback, incentives and system structure, using a lightweight recursive questioning technique influenced by—but deliberately distinct from—5 Whys.
{% endhint %}

***

## Context

An organisation observes a recurring behaviour that is widely regarded as undesirable, ineffective, or misaligned with stated intent.

Examples include:

* deterministic planning in high-uncertainty contexts
* excessive estimation and forecasting
* defensive status reporting
* risk avoidance and slow decision-making
* persistent breaches of work-in-progress limits
* local optimisation that degrades system outcomes
* improvement work being repeatedly displaced by urgent delivery

Despite repeated attempts to change the behaviour through guidance, training, new practices, or expectation-setting, it persists.

Persistence is information.

Before concluding that the behaviour itself is the problem, the organisation should ask what conditions make it a reasonable response to the system in which people are operating.

***

## Problem

The organisation treats observed behaviour as the problem.

As a result:

* people are coached, encouraged, or pressured to behave differently
* new practices are introduced alongside existing incentives
* compliance is monitored rather than constraints examined
* individuals or teams are held responsible for systemic patterns
* frustration increases when the expected behavioural change does not occur

The intervention attempts to change behaviour while leaving intact the conditions that make the behaviour rational.

The behaviour therefore persists, disappears temporarily, or re-emerges in another form.

***

## Forces at Play

People make decisions within systems of incentives, constraints, expectations, dependencies, and consequences.

They tend to optimise for what the system makes safe, credible, legitimate, rewarded, or necessary.

For example:

* the system rewards predictability, narrative coherence, or risk deflection
* learning is treated as secondary to commitment
* uncertainty is implicitly punished
* governance relies on forward certainty rather than emerging evidence
* decision rights sit elsewhere despite expectations of team autonomy
* dependencies make starting work easier than finishing it
* escalation carries greater personal risk than accepting additional work
* performance measures reward local outcomes over system outcomes
* approval mechanisms make waiting costly
* information arrives too late for decisions to remain reversible

Under these conditions, apparently undesirable behaviour may be the least risky or most rational option available.

Persistent behaviour should therefore be treated as a signal about the system before it is treated as evidence of individual failure.

***

## Solution

Apply **Behaviour → Constraint Mapping**.

Begin with an observed behaviour and systematically investigate the conditions that make that behaviour rational.

The central question is:

> **What constraints make this behaviour the safest, most rewarded, or least risky choice?**

The inquiry can be informed by causal techniques such as 5 Whys, but it is not conventional root-cause analysis.

The objective is not to drill down through a linear causal chain until a single root cause is found.

Instead, each question expands the causal field, revealing interacting system conditions and progressively crossing organisational boundaries.

One critical rule applies throughout:

**Answers must reference system conditions, not people.**

The mapping is therefore iterative:

Observe → Map → Hypothesise → Intervene → Observe

***

## Structure

### Step 1: Name the Behaviour Neutrally

Describe what is happening without embedding an explanation or judgement.

For example:

> “Work-in-progress frequently exceeds the team's agreed WIP limit.”

Rather than:

> “Teams ignore their WIP limits.”

Or:

> “Teams perform detailed estimation and create sequenced delivery plans before significant learning has occurred.”

Rather than:

> “Teams spend too much time planning.”

At this point the behaviour is an observation, not yet a problem.

Its persistence is evidence that something in the surrounding system may be reinforcing it.

***

### Step 2: Ask Constraint-Focused “Why” Questions

Begin asking why the observed behaviour makes sense within the system.

The questions should progressively widen the field of inquiry rather than converge prematurely on a root cause.

For example:

* Why are WIP limits frequently breached?
* What causes teams to start additional work despite already having reached their agreed limit?
* Why does starting new work become the rational response?
* What prevents the team from finishing, stopping, delaying, or rejecting existing or incoming work instead?
* Why can't the team remove or control those constraints itself?
* What dependencies, policies, decision rights, specialist capabilities, approvals, commitments, or external demands lie outside the team's control?
* Why does the wider organisation create or preserve those conditions?
* What governance expectations, incentives, funding mechanisms, risk structures, performance measures, or coordination mechanisms reinforce them?
* What would happen if people behaved differently?
* What consequences would a team or individual experience if they refused additional work, exposed uncertainty, challenged a commitment, delayed a request, or otherwise acted contrary to the observed behaviour?

The questions are **not** a fixed sequence and do not need to stop at five.

Continue until the inquiry has sufficiently exposed the system conditions that make the behaviour rational.

Look particularly for:

* incentives
* governance expectations
* risk distribution
* decision rights
* approval mechanisms
* dependencies
* information latency
* performance measures
* commitment mechanisms
* legitimacy signals

Do **not** stop simply because the inquiry reaches another organisational boundary.

“Leadership wants it”, “Finance requires it”, “Risk won't approve it”, or “the Product Owner keeps adding work” are not terminal explanations.

They identify the next part of the system to investigate.

***

### Step 3: Identify Constraint Classes

Group the conditions that emerge from the inquiry.

Common constraint classes include:

* funding and investment rules
* governance and review forums
* performance evaluation mechanisms
* reporting and visibility expectations
* dependency and coordination structures
* decision rights and approval mechanisms
* risk ownership and escalation mechanisms
* timing of commitment relative to learning
* information availability and latency
* organisational boundaries and specialist capabilities

The purpose is not to create an exhaustive taxonomy; it is to develop a sufficiently useful explanation of why the observed behaviour makes sense.

Multiple constraints may interact to reinforce the same behaviour. There may be no single root cause.

***

### Step 4: Reframe the Behaviour

Step 4: Reframe the Behaviour

Replace the original framing:

> “This is undesirable behaviour that people need to stop.”

with:

> “This behaviour is a rational response to these conditions.”

This does not remove individual agency or accountability; it changes the focus of the inquiry from correcting individuals to understanding the conditions within which their choices are being made.

The organisation can now ask a more useful question:

> What would need to be different about the system for a different behaviour to become rational?

***

### Step 5: Select a Constraint to Adjust

Do **not** attempt to redesign the entire system.

Instead, ask:

* Which constraint most strongly reinforces the behaviour?
* Which constraint appears to make alternative behaviour particularly costly or risky?
* Which constraint could be adjusted with relatively little disruption?
* What small change might alter the choices available?
* What system signal would indicate that the change mattered?

Interventions target **constraints** rather than prescribing behaviour.

The aim is not to tell people to make a different choice.

It is to **change the conditions** under which the choice is made.

***

### Step 6: Treat the Intervention as a Hypothesis

Changing a constraint does not guarantee that behaviour will change as expected.

Treat the intervention as a hypothesis:

> If this constraint is materially contributing to the observed behaviour, changing it should alter the behaviour or associated system signals.

Observe what happens.

If the behaviour changes, the intervention provides evidence that the constraint was significant.

If the behaviour persists, the mapping may be incomplete, another constraint may dominate, or the relationship between the constraint and behaviour may have been misunderstood.

Update the map accordingly.

Behaviour → Constraint Mapping is therefore recursive rather than deterministic:

**Observe → Map → Hypothesise → Intervene → Observe → Update**

***

## Resulting Context

When a meaningful constraint changes, different choices may become rational without requiring behavioural enforcement.

As a result:

* new behaviours may emerge without instruction
* previously persistent behaviours may become unnecessary
* resistance may decrease
* uncertainty may become safer to expose
* learning may become a legitimate input to decisions
* governance conversations may shift from blame toward system design
* previously hidden organisational trade-offs may become explicit

However, identifying a constraint does not mean the organisation will choose to change it.

The inquiry may reveal that the organisation values something produced by the constraint more highly than the behaviour it claims to want.

For example, an organisation may say it wants faster decision-making while maintaining approval mechanisms designed to minimise risk.

Behaviour → Constraint Mapping makes that trade-off visible.

The organisation can then choose to change the constraint, accept the behaviour it produces, or explicitly acknowledge the trade-off it is making.

***

## Anti-Patterns

#### Stopping at an Organisational Boundary

Accepting explanations such as:

* “because leadership wants it”
* “because Finance requires it”
* “because Risk won't approve it”
* “because the Product Owner keeps adding work”

These statements identify another part of the system to investigate; they do not complete the inquiry.

#### Concluding with “Culture” or “Mindset”

Culture and mindset may describe observed patterns, but using them as terminal explanations simply relocates the problem into people's attitudes.

Ask what system conditions make those attitudes adaptive.

#### Searching for a Root Cause

Behaviour in complex organisations rarely has a single linear cause.

The objective is to understand interacting constraints sufficiently well to form a useful intervention hypothesis, not to identify a definitive root cause.

#### Replacing Constraint Change with Behavioural Instruction

Training, communication, coaching, new ceremonies, or expectation-setting may be useful, but they will rarely overcome conditions that continue to reward the existing behaviour.

#### Treating the Map as Truth

A Behaviour → Constraint Map is an explanatory hypothesis, not a definitive model of the organisation.

Its usefulness is tested through intervention and observation.

#### Measuring Behavioural Compliance

Success is not demonstrated because people appear to follow the newly preferred behaviour.

Observe whether the underlying system signals change and whether the new behaviour remains rational without continued enforcement.

***

## EOM Alignment

This pattern directly enacts the core EOM principle:

> To change behaviour, you must change the system that makes that behaviour rational.

It assumes:

* behaviour is information
* persistent behaviour is evidence about system conditions
* local optimisation can be rational while producing poor system outcomes
* uncertainty and risk shape behaviour
* constraints interact rather than forming simple causal chains
* learning requires structural permission
* interventions are hypotheses
* system change must itself be subject to learning

Behaviour → Constraint Mapping therefore treats organisational behaviour not primarily as something to control, but as something from which to learn.

***

## Known Uses

This pattern is commonly applied to:

* planning and estimation practices
* work-in-progress limit breaches
* status reporting and governance forums
* risk escalation behaviours
* backlog and prioritisation conflicts
* improvement work starvation
* defensive metric usage
* slow decision-making
* dependency management
* excessive approval processes
* local optimisation
* resistance to experimentation

***

## Closing Note

Behaviour → Constraint Mapping does not tell organisations what to do.

It makes visible what the system is already doing.

The essential question is not:

> How do we stop people behaving this way?

It is:

> What makes behaving this way make sense?

Once those conditions are visible, the organisation can make an explicit choice: change the constraint, accept the behaviour it produces, or acknowledge the trade-off it is making.
