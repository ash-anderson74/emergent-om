# Why Behaviour Follows Systems

The Emergent Operating Model (EOM) is anchored on a simple system-level premise:

> **People behave rationally within the constraints of the operating system they inhabit.**

This is neither a slogan nor a philosophical position. It is a descriptive claim about how human behaviour reliably emerges in organised systems. Understanding why this holds — and where it comes from — is important, particularly for readers who want to know whether this assumption is merely intuitive or grounded in something more fundamental.

***

#### What Kind of Claim This Is

The Emergent Operating Model anchor is not a moral claim about how people ought to behave, nor a motivational claim about intent, culture, or values. It makes no assertions about intelligence, goodwill, or effort.

It is a **systems claim**.

Specifically, it describes regularities in behaviour that appear when people operate under persistent constraints: incentives, feedback loops, decision rights, risk placement, information availability, and governance mechanisms.

In this view, behaviour is not the primary variable to be managed. It is an outcome produced by the interaction between people and the system in which they operate.

That distinction matters. It shifts attention away from asking whether people are sufficiently motivated, disciplined, aligned, or informed, and toward asking what conditions make the observed behaviour reasonable.

***

#### Convergence Across Disciplines

What gives this anchor its strength is not originality, but convergence. Variations of the same idea appear repeatedly across disciplines concerned with human behaviour, organisations, decision-making, safety, and systems.

In sociology, behaviour has long been understood as shaped by institutional structures, social roles, norms, and power. Agency exists, but it is exercised within conditions that make some actions easier, safer, or more legitimate than others. Persistent organisational behaviour is therefore difficult to explain purely through individual disposition.

Economics approaches the problem from a different direction. Individuals respond to incentives, information, scarcity, and risk. Herbert Simon's concept of **bounded rationality** challenged the idea that people optimise against complete information or unlimited cognitive capacity. Decisions are instead made with the information, time, and options available locally. Behaviour that appears suboptimal from outside the situation can therefore be entirely reasonable from within it.

Psychology reinforces this view. Human decision-making is affected by cognitive limits, time pressure, social risk, workload, loss aversion, and perceived consequences. What appears irrational in the abstract may make considerable sense once the circumstances in which the choice was made are understood.

Quality management provides an especially important organisational precedent. W. Edwards Deming repeatedly argued that performance cannot be understood independently of the system within which people work. Persistent patterns of poor performance should therefore lead management to examine the design of the system rather than defaulting to explanations based on individual effort or competence.

Systems thinking develops the same proposition at a broader level. Thinkers such as Donella Meadows and Peter Senge describe how recurring patterns of behaviour emerge from system structure: feedback loops, rules, information flows, delays, incentives, goals, and relationships. Systems are ultimately revealed by the patterns they repeatedly produce, not merely by their stated intent.

Modern safety science makes the connection to behaviour particularly explicit. The principle of **local rationality**, strongly associated with the work of Sidney Dekker and related Human and Organisational Performance thinking, asks us to understand why an action made sense to the people involved given the goals, pressures, information, risks, and constraints they faced at the time.

This matters because retrospective judgement has access to information that the person acting did not. An action may appear obviously mistaken once its consequences are known while having been a reasonable choice within the circumstances in which it occurred.

Related work in resilience engineering and Safety-II, including that of Erik Hollnagel, similarly draws attention to the difference between **work as imagined** and **work as done**. People continually adapt formal processes to real conditions. Those adaptations are not merely deviations to eliminate; they are information about how the system actually functions.

Organisational design and operations research apply these principles every day, often implicitly. Funding models shape priorities. Metrics shape optimisation. Governance shapes risk-taking. Decision rights shape autonomy. Reporting structures shape ownership. Information latency shapes commitment. Approval mechanisms shape responsiveness.

EOM does not claim these foundations as original.

The wording of its anchor is its own synthesis, but the underlying proposition stands on a long-established body of thought showing that behaviour is shaped by system structure, local information, incentives, risk, and constraint.

EOM connects these ideas and makes their implication explicit:

> **Persistent behaviour should first be treated as information about the system that produced it.**

***

#### Why This Is Not a Truism

At first glance, the anchor can feel obvious. That does not make it a truism.

A truism is true by definition. This proposition contradicts several deeply embedded assumptions in management thinking, including the belief that:

* behaviour changes primarily through instruction or training
* resistance explains failed change
* better communication necessarily produces alignment
* compliance leads to learning
* poor outcomes imply inadequate individual performance

EOM rejects these as sufficient explanations for persistent organisational behaviour.

If a behaviour repeatedly survives training, coaching, new processes, new terminology, new structures, and renewed expectations, its persistence is itself information.

Something in the surrounding system may still make that behaviour useful, safe, rewarded, legitimate, or necessary.

The important question therefore becomes less:

> _“Why won't people behave differently?”_

and more:

> **“What conditions make behaving this way make sense?”**

Like other foundational systems insights — such as the distinction between local and global optimisation, or the role of feedback latency in risk — this can feel obvious once articulated. Its implications are much less commonly followed through.

***

#### Could It Be Otherwise?

For the anchor to be substantially wrong as an explanation of persistent organisational behaviour, one or more of the following would need to hold reliably:

* people would need to act consistently against incentives and perceived risk
* behaviour would need to remain largely unaffected by feedback
* organisational structure would need to have little influence on choices
* instruction would need to override conflicting constraints
* decision rights would need to have little influence on behaviour
* power and risk distribution would need to be behaviourally irrelevant
* local information would need to have little effect on decision-making

These conditions do not hold reliably in real organisations.

This does not mean people have no agency, that every individual action is rational, or that behaviour can always be predicted from structure.

Nor does describing behaviour as rational mean that it is desirable, correct, ethical, or globally optimal.

The claim is narrower.

Persistent patterns of behaviour are often better understood by examining the conditions under which they occur than by attributing them primarily to individual attitude, competence, or intent.

***

#### Beyond Technology

Although EOM is framed for complex product environments, the anchor applies far beyond technology.

The same dynamics are visible in healthcare, education, finance, government, journalism, academia, law, manufacturing, and safety-critical industries.

Clinicians adapt procedures around workload and resource constraints. Teachers respond to assessment incentives. Managers optimise against performance measures. Regulators respond to the risks attached to their decisions. Journalists respond to attention economics and publication deadlines. Engineers adapt formal procedures to the realities of getting work done.

In each case, behaviour that produces undesirable system-level outcomes may nevertheless make sense locally.

Technology environments simply make many of these dynamics particularly visible because feedback can be rapid, work is often observable, and organisational constraints frequently intersect with explicit delivery processes.

***

#### Why the Wording Matters

Many theories gesture toward this idea in different ways. EOM makes it explicit and places it at the centre of the operating model.

The phrasing is deliberate:

* _Rational_ asks us to understand behaviour in its local context before judging it
* _Constraints_ focuses attention on the conditions shaping available choices
* _Operating system_ names the otherwise largely invisible combination of governance, incentives, structures, decision rights, information flows, funding mechanisms, and expectations
* _Inhabit_ acknowledges that people rarely choose all of the conditions within which they are expected to operate

This framing shifts attention away from behaviour correction and toward system design.

It does not deny individual responsibility.

It simply recognises that persistent patterns across teams, roles, or time are unlikely to be adequately explained by repeatedly locating the problem in the individuals displaying them.

***

#### From Anchor to Practice

The practical implication of the anchor is straightforward.

When an apparently undesirable behaviour persists, do not begin by asking how to stop it.

Treat the behaviour as a signal.

Ask what information it contains about the surrounding system.

What makes this behaviour safer than the alternative?

What is rewarded?

What risk does it avoid?

What decisions lie elsewhere?

What happens if uncertainty is exposed?

What dependencies make another course of action difficult?

What organisational conditions make this response reasonable?

This is the basis of the EOM **Behaviour to Constraint Mapping** pattern.

The pattern does not introduce a new theory of organisational behaviour. It operationalises the systems perspective described here by providing a lightweight diagnostic for following persistent behaviour outward into the constraints that sustain it.

The aim is not to identify someone to correct, nor to discover a single root cause.

It is to understand enough of the surrounding system to ask:

> **What would need to change for a different behaviour to become rational?**

Any subsequent intervention can then be treated as a hypothesis rather than a prescription.

If the identified constraint materially contributes to the behaviour, changing it should change what the system produces.

If it does not, the organisation has learned something about its model of the system.

***

#### Conclusion

The Emergent Operating Model anchor is not a novel theory of human behaviour.

It is a compressed synthesis of ideas that have developed across systems thinking, quality management, economics, organisational theory, psychology, resilience engineering, and safety science.

Its value lies not in claiming those ideas as new, but in centring their implication for organisational change:

> **To change behaviour, you must change the system that makes that behaviour rational.**

EOM therefore does not treat persistent behaviour primarily as something to correct.

It treats it as information.

If the same behaviour continues to emerge despite repeated attempts to change it, the first question should not be what is wrong with the people exhibiting it.

The more useful question is:

> **What is the system telling us?**

EOM does not invent this idea.

It centres it — and then refuses to let improvement efforts ignore it.

That refusal is the foundation on which everything else in EOM rests.
