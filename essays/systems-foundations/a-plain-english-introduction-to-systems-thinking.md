# A Plain-English Introduction to Systems Thinking

## A Plain-English Introduction to Systems Thinking

Much of the frustration experienced in organisations begins with a simple mistake: we explain recurring problems in terms of people when the more important explanation often lies in the system around them.

A team misses a commitment, so we question its discipline. A transformation fails to produce the expected change, so we talk about resistance. A decision turns out badly, so we look for the person who made it. A process is not followed, so we conclude that people need more training.

Sometimes those explanations are relevant. They are rarely sufficient.

Systems thinking begins from a different premise. Rather than asking first what is wrong with the people involved, it asks what conditions made their behaviour sensible. It treats behaviour not simply as an expression of attitude, competence, or intent, but as something produced through interaction with structures, incentives, information, constraints, risks, and feedback.

That shift in perspective is fundamental to the Emergent Operating Model.

***

#### Seeing the System Behind the Behaviour

When organisations talk about “the system”, the term can sound abstract. In practice, the system is simply the environment within which people are expected to make decisions and get work done.

It includes formal things: reporting lines, governance forums, budgets, performance measures, approval processes, policies, and decision rights. But it also includes less visible conditions: who is blamed when something goes wrong, what kinds of uncertainty are tolerated, how quickly feedback arrives, whether bad news is safe to communicate, which commitments can be renegotiated, and which cannot.

Taken together, these conditions form the practical operating environment of the organisation.

They matter because people learn very quickly how that environment really works.

A company may say that it values experimentation, while failure damages careers. It may encourage teams to move quickly while requiring several layers of approval. It may promote autonomy while retaining important decisions elsewhere. It may claim to value outcomes while rewarding people for hitting delivery dates.

In each case, the formal message and the practical system point in different directions.

When that happens, behaviour tends to follow the system.

***

#### Why Individual Explanations Are So Seductive

Individual explanations are attractive because they are simple.

If a team is missing deadlines, it is easy to conclude that the team needs better planning. If people do not adopt a new practice, it is easy to describe them as resistant. If managers continue to demand detailed forecasts despite repeated uncertainty, it is tempting to attribute this to mindset or culture.

These explanations are satisfying because they identify something that appears manageable. Train the team. Coach the manager. Change the behaviour. Communicate the strategy again.

But persistent patterns should make us suspicious of explanations based primarily on individual shortcomings.

If capable people repeatedly make the same apparently poor decisions, across different teams and over long periods of time, then it becomes increasingly implausible that the problem lies simply in the people themselves. More often, those people are adapting to recurring conditions.

Systems thinking therefore asks a different question:

> **What makes this behaviour a sensible choice in the circumstances?**

That question does not excuse poor decisions or remove individual responsibility. It simply asks us to understand the context before judging the behaviour.

***

#### Rational Behaviour Can Still Produce Poor Outcomes

One of the most important insights in systems thinking is that behaviour can be rational locally while producing undesirable outcomes globally.

Consider estimation.

A team may know that a piece of work is highly uncertain, yet still provide a confident date. From outside, this may look irresponsible. But suppose funding depends on the date. Suppose a governance forum expects a commitment. Suppose saying “we do not know yet” is interpreted as lack of preparation. Suppose every neighbouring team is constructing its own plan around the answer.

Under those conditions, producing an estimate is not necessarily irrational. It may be the safest and most credible option available.

The same principle appears elsewhere.

A team may accept more work despite already having too much in progress because refusing an urgent request carries more risk than breaching a WIP limit.

A status report may become overly optimistic because uncertainty attracts scrutiny while confidence creates temporary safety.

A manager may delay a decision because approval mechanisms distribute the consequences of action more heavily than the consequences of waiting.

Each behaviour may be undesirable when viewed from the perspective of the whole organisation. Yet each can still make sense to the person or team making the choice.

This is the tension between local optimisation and system outcomes.

Individuals and teams do not need to be irrational for the system to behave badly. Quite the opposite: poor system outcomes can emerge from many people behaving quite rationally within the conditions they face.

***

#### What the System Rewards Matters More Than What It Says

Organisations often attempt to influence behaviour through statements of intent.

They publish values. They introduce new frameworks. They run training programmes. Leaders talk about empowerment, experimentation, collaboration, ownership, and customer focus.

None of this is inherently useless.

But people eventually learn the difference between what the organisation says matters and what the organisation actually rewards.

If deployment frequency affects bonuses, deployment frequency will receive attention.

If delivery dates dominate governance conversations, teams will optimise around delivery dates.

If managers are punished for failed experiments but not for missed opportunities, experimentation will remain cautious.

If teams are told to collaborate but measured entirely on local performance, collaboration will remain secondary.

The operating system is revealed not by slogans but by consequences.

This is why behaviour change programmes so often disappoint. They try to install new behaviours on top of old conditions.

The language changes. The workshops happen. The new terminology appears in presentations. But the incentives, risks, decision rights, funding mechanisms, reporting expectations, and approval structures remain largely intact.

Before long, the old behaviour returns.

From a systems perspective, that should not be surprising.

***

#### Systems Thinking Changes the Question

Traditional change efforts often begin with a behavioural objective:

> How do we get people to do something different?

Systems thinking turns the question around:

> **What would need to change for different behaviour to make sense?**

That is a much more demanding question because it moves attention away from the people displaying the behaviour and towards the organisation that created the conditions around them.

Suppose teams repeatedly start new work before existing work is complete. A conventional response might reinforce WIP limits, improve Kanban training, or ask managers to hold teams accountable.

A systems response begins elsewhere.

What prevents existing work from finishing? Are teams dependent on decisions they cannot make? Are specialist skills scarce? Can senior stakeholders bypass prioritisation? Are people rewarded for responsiveness rather than completion? Is rejecting incoming work politically unsafe? Does blocked work remain counted against the team while new work continues to arrive?

The apparent behavioural problem becomes a source of information.

Instead of asking how to enforce the WIP limit more effectively, we begin to understand why breaching it is rational.

That is the essence of systems thinking in practice.

***

#### The System Is More Than Structure

It is tempting to equate systems thinking with organisation design: reporting lines, team boundaries, or formal processes.

Those things matter, but the system extends much further.

Information is part of the system. A decision made before relevant evidence becomes available will behave differently from one made afterwards.

Time is part of the system. Long feedback loops allow assumptions to survive longer and make commitments harder to reverse.

Risk is part of the system. Whoever carries the consequences of failure will behave differently from someone protected from them.

Legitimacy is part of the system. People learn which kinds of evidence are taken seriously and which are dismissed.

Power is part of the system. Decision rights shape what people can genuinely influence, regardless of how autonomous they are told they should be.

Measures are part of the system. The moment a metric becomes consequential, people have reason to optimise around it.

These conditions interact. They do not usually form a neat sequence of cause and effect.

That is another reason systems thinking resists simple root-cause explanations. Organisational behaviour is often produced by several mutually reinforcing conditions rather than one isolated defect.

***

#### Where the Emergent Operating Model Fits

The Emergent Operating Model is built explicitly on this perspective.

It is anchored on the premise:

> **People behave rationally within the constraints of the operating system they inhabit.**

That premise changes what an operating model is expected to do.

Rather than prescribing the correct behaviour in greater detail, EOM asks whether the surrounding conditions make desirable behaviour possible and rational.

This is why it places so much emphasis on learning before commitment, reversible decisions under uncertainty, governance as sensing rather than control, and the design of constraints rather than the multiplication of instructions.

The intention is not to remove structure. Complex organisations need structure.

Nor is it to assume that every behaviour should simply be tolerated because it can be explained systemically.

The point is that persistent behaviour should first be treated as evidence.

If an organisation repeatedly observes a behaviour it does not want, the first question should not be how to suppress it. The organisation should ask what conditions continue to produce it.

Only then does intervention become meaningful.

***

#### A Different View of Accountability

Systems thinking is sometimes misunderstood as a way of removing personal accountability.

It does the opposite.

It makes accountability more demanding because it refuses to stop at the nearest individual.

People remain responsible for choices they can reasonably make. Leaders remain responsible for judgement. Teams remain responsible for how they conduct their work.

But the organisation also becomes accountable for the environment within which those choices occur.

If a system repeatedly rewards local optimisation, punishes uncertainty, delays feedback, concentrates decision rights, and distributes risk unevenly, then continually asking individuals to overcome those conditions through better behaviour is not accountability. It is avoidance.

A systems view therefore broadens the object of responsibility.

It asks not only whether people made good decisions, but whether the organisation made good decisions about the conditions under which those people were expected to operate.

***

#### From Behaviour to Learning

Once behaviour is treated as information, recurring organisational problems become more interesting.

A breached WIP limit tells us something.

A defensive status report tells us something.

A reluctance to experiment tells us something.

A persistent demand for precise forecasts in an uncertain environment tells us something.

The question is what.

That is why EOM treats observed behaviour as a signal rather than merely a deviation from expectation. A persistent pattern is an invitation to investigate the conditions surrounding it.

The practical inquiry becomes:

> What makes this behaviour safe?

> What makes the alternative risky?

> What is rewarded?

> What information is missing or delayed?

> Where do the relevant decision rights sit?

> What would happen if someone behaved differently?

These questions move the conversation away from blame and towards understanding.

And once the conditions become visible, it becomes possible to change them deliberately and observe what happens next.

***

#### In Closing

Systems thinking is not primarily a collection of diagrams, terminology, or modelling techniques.

At its most practical, it is a discipline of explanation.

It asks us to resist the instinct to attribute recurring organisational problems to the character or competence of the people nearest to them, and instead examine the environment that continually shapes their choices.

Behaviour still matters. People still matter. Individual judgement still matters.

But persistent behaviour does not occur in a vacuum.

It emerges from a system of incentives, risks, information, constraints, permissions, dependencies, and consequences.

The Emergent Operating Model begins there because lasting organisational change cannot be achieved simply by describing a different way for people to behave.

If you want different behaviour, you first need to understand what makes the existing behaviour make sense.

Only then can you begin to change the system that sustains it.
