# Why Psychological Safety Emerges — or Disappears — by Design

## Why Psychological Safety Emerges — or Disappears — by Design

Psychological safety is often discussed as a cultural quality: something leaders should encourage, model, or cultivate through the way they communicate and behave. There is truth in that, and leadership behaviour matters enormously. But the Emergent Operating Model takes a broader view. Psychological safety is not created by encouragement alone, nor can it be sustained by good intent in a system that makes candour personally costly.

In complex organisations, psychological safety is better understood as something the operating system either **permits or suppresses**. People learn very quickly whether uncertainty can be exposed safely, whether bad news is welcomed or punished, whether challenging a commitment is legitimate, whether failed experiments are treated as learning or incompetence, and whether raising risk early creates protection or scrutiny. Those lessons are rarely learned from values statements; they are learned from consequences.

***

#### Psychological Safety Is About Interpersonal Risk

Psychological safety does not mean that people feel comfortable all the time, that disagreement disappears, or that performance expectations become less demanding. At its core, it concerns whether people believe they can take interpersonal risks without suffering disproportionate negative consequences.

Can someone say:

> “I don't know.”

Can they admit:

> “Our assumption was wrong.”

Can they challenge a plan that has already attracted executive support?

Can they expose a risk without becoming the owner of the problem?

Can they stop work that no longer appears valuable?

Can they disagree with someone who controls their progression, funding, or reputation?

These are not abstract cultural questions. They are practical questions about risk, and risk is shaped by the system.

***

#### Behaviour Is Rational Within the System

EOM rests on a simple anchor:

> **People behave rationally within the constraints of the operating system they inhabit.**

Seen through that lens, silence, defensiveness, excessive optimism, blame-shifting, and risk aversion should not immediately be interpreted as failures of character. They may be adaptations to the environment.

Suppose a team has repeatedly learned that exposing uncertainty leads to interrogation while confident commitments receive approval. Suppose missed estimates are remembered during performance reviews, while the assumptions behind them are forgotten. Suppose escalating a risk transfers ownership of the risk to the person who raised it. Suppose cancelling work requires more justification than continuing it.

Under those circumstances, confidence becomes safer than uncertainty, silence becomes safer than challenge, and continuing becomes safer than stopping. The behaviour may be undesirable from the perspective of the organisation as a whole, but entirely rational from the perspective of the individual or team making the choice.

That distinction matters. Psychological safety does not disappear simply because people lack courage; it can disappear because the system makes courage unnecessarily expensive.

***

#### Safety Is Revealed by What Happens After Someone Speaks

An organisation can declare that people should “speak up”, but the meaningful question is what happens when they actually do:

What happens when someone reports that a delivery date is unrealistic?

What happens when a team says that it cannot yet estimate something credibly?

What happens when an experiment disproves an assumption behind a major initiative?

What happens when an engineer challenges an architectural decision made by someone more senior?

What happens when a product team recommends stopping work into which significant money and reputation have already been invested?

The answer to those questions tells us far more about psychological safety than a survey question or leadership statement. People observe consequences: they notice who gets praised, who gets challenged, what gets escalated, what gets remembered, and what is quietly discouraged. Over time, they adapt. The system teaches people what is safe to say.

***

#### Psychological Safety as an Emergent Property

This is why EOM treats psychological safety less as a value to install and more as an **emergent property of a system capable of learning**. An organisation that genuinely depends on learning must make the exposure of incomplete knowledge legitimate, and that requires more than telling people to be open.

If hypotheses are genuinely provisional, disproving them must not be treated as failure.

If commitments are proportional to knowledge, saying “we do not know yet” must sometimes be an acceptable answer.

If governance exists to sense and adapt, bad news must be useful information rather than evidence of non-compliance.

If measures are used diagnostically, variation should trigger inquiry rather than judgement.

If decisions remain reversible where uncertainty is high, changing direction must not automatically be interpreted as poor planning.

Under those conditions, many of the behaviours associated with psychological safety become rational. People surface risks earlier because doing so improves the decision rather than threatening their credibility; teams invalidate assumptions because evidence matters more than preserving the original narrative; leaders hear bad news sooner because there is less incentive to delay it; and people ask for help before failure becomes unavoidable. Learning accelerates not because everyone has been instructed to “feel safe”, but because telling the truth is less dangerous than concealing it.

***

#### Psychological Safety and Accountability Are Not Opposites

Psychological safety is sometimes caricatured as the absence of consequences, which would make it incompatible with accountability. But that is not what psychological safety requires. A psychologically safe environment can still have demanding standards, difficult feedback, strong professional expectations, and real accountability for negligent or unethical behaviour. The distinction is between accountability for what someone could reasonably control and punishment for revealing information the organisation needs.

A team should be accountable for concealing a known material risk, but it should not be punished for surfacing that risk early. An engineer can be held accountable for poor professional judgement without becoming less credible merely because an experiment disproved their hypothesis. A leader can challenge weak reasoning without creating conditions in which disagreement itself becomes hazardous.

Accountability without psychological safety can become performative, because people learn to defend their position rather than reveal what is actually happening. Safety without accountability is equally problematic. The useful condition is one in which people can expose reality while remaining responsible for how they respond to it.

***

#### Why Behavioural Programmes Often Have Limited Effect

Because psychological safety is commonly framed as a cultural issue, organisations often respond to its absence through behavioural interventions: they run workshops, introduce team norms, encourage leaders to show vulnerability, and train people in giving feedback, active listening, or speaking up. These interventions can help. Leadership behaviour genuinely affects interpersonal risk, and better conversational practices can change how people experience a team. But they have limits.

Imagine asking leaders to welcome uncertainty while the funding process requires fixed annual commitments.

Imagine encouraging teams to admit failure while performance evaluation rewards delivery against plan.

Imagine asking people to escalate risk early while governance turns every escalation into an investigation.

Imagine telling teams they are empowered while significant decisions still require approval elsewhere.

The behavioural message says one thing while the operating system says another, and people eventually learn which one matters. The problem is not necessarily that the training failed; it is that the surrounding system continued to make the old behaviour rational.

***

#### Leadership Matters Because Leaders Shape Conditions

This does not make leadership irrelevant. Quite the opposite: leaders are among the most powerful mechanisms through which the operating system is experienced. The significance of leadership behaviour is not merely symbolic; leaders influence what carries risk and what carries legitimacy.

A leader who responds to bad news with curiosity rather than immediate correction changes the cost of raising problems. A leader who says “we do not know yet” in a governance forum legitimises uncertainty. A leader who allows evidence to overturn their preferred solution reduces the risk associated with dissent, while a leader who distinguishes learning from performance evaluation makes experimentation safer. Protecting people who expose uncomfortable information similarly demonstrates that candour is not merely rhetorically encouraged. Repeated consistently, these behaviours affect the system.

Leadership can, of course, create the opposite conditions just as easily.

A single highly visible punishment for speaking inconveniently can outweigh months of encouragement to “speak up”. A leader who publicly asks for challenge but privately penalises dissent creates an especially dangerous system because stated expectations and actual consequences diverge. People will generally learn from the consequences.

***

#### Governance Can Create Fear Without Anyone Intending To

Psychological safety can also disappear without any individual leader behaving maliciously. A governance system may create fear structurally.

Suppose a portfolio review is designed around commitments and variance from plan. Nobody needs to explicitly punish uncertainty. The structure already makes certainty more legitimate.

Suppose risk forums require named ownership of every issue. People may delay surfacing poorly understood risks until they are confident enough to defend them.

Suppose funding decisions depend on confident business cases. Teams learn that acknowledging uncertainty threatens investment.

Suppose performance systems reward successful delivery but do not distinguish between intelligent experimentation and avoidable failure. People become more conservative.

Nobody necessarily intended to create fear, yet the system has redistributed interpersonal and professional risk in ways that make candour less rational. That is why psychological safety cannot be understood solely as a question of leadership style; it is also a question of operating-model design.

***

#### Alignment With Existing Research

This view is consistent with the established research on psychological safety, particularly the work of Amy Edmondson, which describes psychological safety as a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking and links it closely to learning behaviour, speaking up, error reporting, and adaptation. The important point is not that fear disappears, but that people do not need to protect themselves from ordinary acts of learning: asking questions, admitting uncertainty, reporting mistakes, challenging assumptions, or proposing alternatives.

EOM adds a systems interpretation to that insight.

If interpersonal risk is experienced consistently across a team or organisation, then the useful question is not only whether individual leaders are behaving appropriately. It is also:

> **What features of the operating system distribute that risk?**

That question directs attention to funding, governance, measures, incentives, decision rights, commitment mechanisms, reporting structures, and the treatment of uncertainty. Psychological safety remains a social phenomenon, but the social conditions within which it emerges are partly designed.

***

#### Psychological Safety as a Diagnostic Signal

For EOM, psychological safety is therefore useful not only as something desirable, but as information about the system. If people routinely hide uncertainty, ask what makes uncertainty costly. If estimates are defended long after their assumptions have weakened, ask what makes revision dangerous. If status reports become progressively more optimistic as problems worsen, ask what happens when bad news moves upward. If experiments rarely invalidate major assumptions, ask whether invalidation carries consequences. If improvement work is continually displaced by urgent delivery, ask what legitimacy improvement has relative to commitment. If teams wait for permission despite being described as autonomous, ask where the real decision rights sit.

These observations do not automatically prove that the organisation lacks psychological safety. They are signals and, as with other persistent behaviours, the useful response is inquiry rather than immediate judgement.

***

#### Psychological Safety Is Contextual

There is another reason to be cautious about treating psychological safety as a singular organisational attribute: people may feel safe in one context and unsafe in another.

A team may be comfortable challenging technical ideas internally while being unwilling to question a portfolio commitment.

An engineer may admit mistakes to peers while avoiding disagreement with a senior executive.

A product manager may freely challenge assumptions during discovery but become defensive once those assumptions have been converted into a funded business case.

Psychological safety therefore does not simply exist or not exist. It can vary by relationship, decision, forum, hierarchy, subject, and consequence. This makes system-level inquiry even more important: rather than asking whether an organisation “has psychological safety”, ask where people experience interpersonal risk and what conditions create it.

***

#### From Safety to System Design

Once psychological safety is viewed in this way, the change question becomes different. Instead of asking:

> _How do we make people feel safer?_

we can ask:

> **What makes telling the truth risky here?**

That inquiry might reveal an approval process, a performance measure, a funding rule, a seniority boundary, a commitment made too early, a governance forum structured around defending plans, a history of punitive responses to failure, or several of these acting together. The intervention then need not begin with another behavioural programme. It can begin by changing one of the conditions that makes concealment, defensiveness, or silence rational and observing what happens next.

***

#### In Closing

Psychological safety cannot simply be installed, nor is it produced merely by declaring that people should speak up, experiment, admit mistakes, or challenge authority. It emerges from repeated experience of what happens when people actually do those things.

An operating system that legitimises uncertainty, treats learning as evidence, allows commitments to change when knowledge changes, aligns authority with accountability, and responds to bad news with inquiry reduces the interpersonal risk attached to truth-telling. Under those conditions, candour becomes more rational; so do challenge, experimentation, and admitting that something did not work.

The Emergent Operating Model therefore does not treat psychological safety primarily as an aspiration to communicate. It treats it as a property to understand through the conditions that produce it.

The most useful question is not:

> _“How do we create psychological safety?”_

It is:

> **“What would need to change for fear, silence, or defensiveness to stop making sense?”**

That question does not guarantee safety, but it directs attention to where meaningful change can occur: the system within which people decide whether speaking honestly is worth the risk.
