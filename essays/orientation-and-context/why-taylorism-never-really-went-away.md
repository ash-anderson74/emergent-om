# Why Taylorism Never Really Went Away

Modern management likes to believe it has moved on.

Over the past century we have seen an almost continuous succession of new management philosophies, each promising to address the shortcomings of its predecessor. Scientific Management gave way to Management by Objectives. Lean challenged mass production. Agile challenged heavyweight project management. DevOps sought to remove the barriers between development and operations. More recently, Product Operating Models, Value Stream Management and artificial intelligence have each promised another step forward in the evolution of organisational performance.

Taken together, they paint the picture of a discipline in constant motion, continually learning from its mistakes.

Yet there is an uncomfortable paradox at the heart of modern organisations. Despite adopting new language, new frameworks and new practices, many continue to behave in remarkably familiar ways. Plans are expected to predict the future with confidence. Estimates become commitments. Governance exists primarily to approve decisions. Teams optimise their own performance even when the wider organisation suffers. Success is frequently measured by how efficiently work is completed rather than by what changes as a result of delivering it.

The vocabulary has changed almost beyond recognition. The assumptions often have not.

Taylorism has become something few organisations consciously advocate, yet many unconsciously practise.

Understanding why requires more than dismissing Taylor's ideas as outdated. It requires understanding why they were so influential in the first place.

### A Philosophy Built for a Different World

Frederick Winslow Taylor was attempting to solve a problem that was both genuine and economically significant.

Industrial production at the turn of the twentieth century was characterised by inconsistency. Skilled workers developed their own techniques. Productivity varied enormously between individuals. Managers possessed little objective understanding of how work was actually performed, while workers often guarded their expertise because it gave them bargaining power. Factories were growing rapidly, but management remained largely informal.

Taylor's response was both elegant and revolutionary.

Rather than relying upon experience or tradition, work should be studied scientifically. Complex activities could be decomposed into repeatable tasks, measured objectively and organised into the most efficient sequence. Once the best method had been identified it could be standardised, taught and repeated consistently across the organisation.

The results were transformative. Productivity increased dramatically throughout manufacturing. Costs fell. Output rose. Industrialisation accelerated.

Scientific Management succeeded because it was exceptionally well suited to the environment in which it was developed.

That point is often forgotten.

Taylor was not wrong.

He solved exactly the problem he set out to solve.

The difficulty is that management theories have a tendency to outlive the conditions that created them.

### The Inheritance We Rarely Notice

Management ideas rarely disappear. They accumulate.

Each generation of thinking builds upon what came before it, preserving some assumptions while rejecting others. As a result, organisations often become philosophical hybrids, adopting new practices without fully abandoning the old mental models that shaped them.

This explains why modern organisations can simultaneously advocate experimentation while demanding certainty, encourage autonomous teams while retaining centralised decision-making, or celebrate agility while measuring success against detailed delivery plans established months earlier.

These contradictions are not necessarily signs of poor leadership. They are the natural consequence of layering successive management philosophies on top of one another without examining whether their assumptions remain compatible.

Taylorism persists not because organisations consciously choose it, but because many of its assumptions have become embedded within management itself. They are no longer recognised as Taylorist ideas. They simply feel like common sense.

Planning should reduce uncertainty. Standardisation improves performance. Variation is undesirable. Managers make decisions; teams execute them. Performance should be measurable. Optimising individual activities improves organisational performance.

Each of these propositions sounds entirely reasonable.

Each also reflects a particular view of how organisations function.

### The Appeal of Certainty

Perhaps the most enduring legacy of Taylorism is not standardisation or measurement, but the belief that uncertainty can largely be eliminated through better planning.

This belief continues to shape almost every aspect of organisational life.

Boards approve annual budgets. Executives commit to delivery roadmaps. Investors expect forecasts. Customers request delivery dates. Regulators require evidence of control. None of these expectations are unreasonable. They reflect legitimate responsibilities within organisations operating under commercial and legal constraints.

The difficulty arises when planning is expected to provide knowledge that does not yet exist.

Knowledge work differs fundamentally from industrial production because much of what matters cannot be known before the work begins. Customer behaviour remains uncertain. Technical constraints emerge unexpectedly. Competitors respond. Markets evolve. Regulations change. Every significant product initiative is, to some degree, an exercise in discovery.

Yet organisations frequently continue to behave as though uncertainty represents incomplete planning rather than incomplete knowledge.

Estimates therefore become commitments.

Plans become promises.

Deviation becomes failure.

The possibility that the original assumptions may simply have been unknowable receives remarkably little attention.

The result is not merely unrealistic planning. It is the gradual creation of systems that discourage learning precisely when learning is most needed.

### Optimising the Wrong Thing

Scientific Management sought to optimise execution because execution was the dominant constraint on industrial performance.

Modern product organisations often inherit that instinct without questioning whether the constraint has changed.

* Delivery becomes faster.
* Processes become leaner.
* Teams increase throughput.
* Deployment frequency improves.

Each achievement is valuable in its own right; none necessarily increases value.

Products rarely fail because software could not be built efficiently enough. More often they fail because organisations misunderstood customer needs, invested in incorrect assumptions, or continued pursuing initiatives long after evidence suggested they should change direction.

The limiting factor is frequently not execution; it is understanding.

Optimising execution when understanding is the primary constraint resembles making a wrong turn more efficiently. Progress becomes increasingly impressive while moving steadily away from the desired destination.

This is one reason modern organisations can exhibit excellent operational performance while consistently disappointing customers.

### Seeing Organisations as Systems

Systems thinking offers a fundamentally different perspective.

Rather than viewing organisational performance as the sum of individual activities, it asks how behaviour emerges from the relationships, incentives, constraints and feedback loops operating throughout the organisation.

This distinction matters enormously.

Traditional management often responds to undesirable behaviour by attempting to change the people. More training. Better leadership. Increased accountability. Stronger governance.

Systems thinking asks a different question.

What conditions make this behaviour the rational response?

If delivery teams consistently overcommit, perhaps the problem is not optimism but incentives.

If estimates repeatedly become commitments, perhaps governance is rewarding certainty rather than honesty.

If local teams optimise their own performance at the expense of organisational outcomes, perhaps measurement systems encourage precisely that behaviour.

People generally behave rationally.

The difficulty is that they behave rationally within the systems they inhabit.

Changing behaviour without changing those systems rarely produces lasting improvement.

### Recognising Taylorism in Modern Organisations

Taylorism seldom announces itself explicitly. It appears through patterns of behaviour that seem entirely ordinary because they have become deeply embedded within management practice.

It is present when governance exists primarily to approve work rather than understand it. It appears when business cases attempt to justify investments with a level of precision unsupported by available evidence. It can be seen when delivery metrics quietly become performance targets, encouraging teams to optimise measures rather than outcomes. It emerges whenever success is defined by completing planned work instead of discovering whether the work was worth doing.

None of these practices are inherently wrong. Indeed, each may be entirely appropriate within stable operational environments where objectives are well understood and variation genuinely represents inefficiency. The danger lies in applying the same assumptions to environments characterised by uncertainty, discovery and continual change.

The question is therefore not whether Taylorism is good or bad.

The question is whether its assumptions remain compatible with the nature of the work.

### The Role of the Emergent Operating Model

The Emergent Operating Model begins from a different premise.

Rather than treating organisations primarily as optimisation systems, it treats them as learning systems.

This is not a rejection of efficiency. Efficient execution remains valuable. Standardisation continues to have an important role wherever work is repetitive and well understood. Governance remains necessary. Planning remains essential.

What changes is the purpose these activities serve.

Planning becomes the articulation of hypotheses rather than declarations of certainty. Governance becomes a mechanism for sensing how the system is behaving rather than approving predetermined decisions. Metrics exist to reveal emerging conditions rather than to define success. Flow matters because it accelerates feedback, and feedback matters because it improves understanding. Decision rights move closer to where evidence is generated, recognising that knowledge frequently emerges during execution rather than before it.

Perhaps most importantly, commitment becomes proportional to knowledge.

Where certainty exists, organisations can and should make strong commitments.

Where uncertainty dominates, preserving options becomes a form of risk management rather than indecision.

These are not procedural adjustments. They represent a fundamentally different philosophy of management.

### Guarding Against an Enduring Legacy

Taylorism will almost certainly never disappear entirely, nor should it.

Factories still exist. Operational processes still benefit from standardisation. Many forms of work remain predictable enough that optimisation produces extraordinary value. Scientific Management remains one of the most influential and successful management philosophies ever developed because it continues to solve the problems for which it was designed.

The challenge facing modern organisations is different.

Increasingly, competitive advantage depends less upon executing known work efficiently than upon discovering what work is worth doing before competitors do. In such environments, learning becomes the scarce resource. The ability to revise assumptions, abandon unsuccessful initiatives and redirect effort rapidly becomes more valuable than the ability to execute an outdated plan flawlessly.

This is where systems thinking offers its greatest contribution. It reminds us that organisational behaviour is rarely the product of individual attitudes alone. It emerges from the structural conditions within which people operate. When those conditions reward certainty, organisations manufacture certainty. When they reward local efficiency, local optimisation flourishes. When they reward learning, adaptation becomes rational.

The Emergent Operating Model is, at its heart, an attempt to create those conditions. It does not seek to replace one management doctrine with another, nor does it dismiss the contributions of those who came before. Instead, it asks a more fundamental question: what kind of system must we create if we want learning, rather than certainty, to become the primary mechanism through which organisations achieve reliable performance?

That question may ultimately prove to be the defining management challenge of the twenty-first century.
