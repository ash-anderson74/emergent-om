# Facilitation Guide: Behaviour Constraint Mapping in Practice

This guide describes how Emergent Operating Model (EOM) operators can facilitate Behaviour → Constraint Mapping. It is not a required method, process, or practice.

***

This guide supports EOM operators in facilitating conversations that use recurring behaviour as evidence about the system.

It is designed to accompany the pattern:

> [**Behaviour to Constraint Mapping**](https://app.gitbook.com/s/yYdXGH2DtxmsDRcRRrNr/16-bahaviour-constraint-mapping)

It is deliberately lightweight. Its purpose is sensemaking: to help a group understand what conditions make an observed behaviour rational before deciding whether anything should change.

It is **not** root-cause analysis, judgement, or solution design.

***

## When to Use This

Use this facilitation approach when you observe:

* persistent behaviours regarded as undesirable, ineffective, or misaligned with intent
* repeated failure of process changes to stick
* defensiveness, blame, or narrative control
* disagreement about “what the real problem is”
* pressure to train, mandate, or enforce behaviour change
* recurring behaviour that appears resistant to intervention

These are signals worth investigating.

The behaviour may be a problem. It may also be a rational adaptation to conditions elsewhere in the system.

The purpose of the session is to find out.

***

## Framing the Session

Begin by establishing a shared premise:

> _We are not here to fix people or defend decisions._\
> _&#x57;e are here to understand what conditions make the current behaviour sensible._

Make it explicit that:

* observed behaviour is evidence, not a verdict
* no behaviour needs to be judged in order to investigate it
* no single root cause is expected
* no solutions are required today
* uncertainty is expected
* learning is the primary outcome

The facilitator should resist framing the session around the behaviour the organisation wants instead; this framing is essential for psychological safety.

The question is not yet:

> “How do we get people to behave differently?”

It is:

> **“What makes behaving this way make sense?”**

***

## Step 1: Name the Behaviour Neutrally

Ask the group to describe **what is happening**, without explaining why it happens or embedding judgement in the description.

Good prompts include:

* “What do we reliably see happening?”
* “What behaviour keeps recurring?”
* “What happens even after we have tried to change it?”
* “What do people actually do when these circumstances arise?”

Capture statements factually, without adjectives that imply motivation, competence, or intent.

For example:

> **“Teams produce detailed delivery plans before validating assumptions.”**

Rather than:

> “Teams spend too much time planning.”

Or:

> **“Work-in-progress frequently exceeds the team's agreed WIP limit.”**

Rather than:

> “Teams ignore their WIP limits.”

At this point, do not assume that the behaviour itself is the problem.

Treat it as an observation about the system.

***

## Step 2: Explore Why the Behaviour Makes Sense

Explicitly redirect the inquiry away from individual motivation.

Instead of:

> “Why are people doing this?”

Ask:

> **“What conditions make this the safest, most reasonable, most legitimate, or least risky option?”**

The inquiry may resemble techniques such as 5 Whys, but the objective is different.

Do **not** drill down in search of a single root cause.

Instead, use each question to expand the causal field and expose more of the system surrounding the behaviour.

Useful prompts include:

* “What makes that response rational?”
* “What prevents them doing something else?”
* “What happens if they don't behave this way?”
* “What risk does this behaviour reduce?”
* “What does the system reward or legitimise?”
* “What would make the alternative difficult?”
* “Who or what sits outside their control?”
* “What conditions preserve that situation?”

If the answer moves to another organisational boundary, follow it.

Statements such as:

* “Leadership wants it.”
* “Finance requires it.”
* “Risk won't approve it.”
* “The Product Owner keeps adding work.”

are not conclusions.

Ask what conditions they are responding to.

The objective is not to assign responsibility to another part of the organisation. It is to continue exposing the system.

***

## Step 3: Surface and Group Constraints

As the inquiry develops, listen for **system conditions** rather than opinions about people.

These may include:

* funding and investment rules
* governance and review expectations
* performance evaluation mechanisms
* reporting and visibility requirements
* decision rights and approval mechanisms
* risk ownership and escalation pathways
* dependency and coordination structures
* specialist capabilities outside the team's control
* timing of commitment relative to learning
* information availability and latency
* incentives and legitimacy signals

Group related conditions where useful.

The purpose is not to produce a comprehensive taxonomy or a perfectly accurate causal model. It is to make enough of the surrounding system visible to explain why the observed behaviour makes sense.

Expect multiple constraints to interact.

Avoid asking:

> “So which one is the root cause?”

There may be no single root cause.

The map is an explanatory hypothesis about the system, not a definitive model of it.

***

## Step 4: Reframe the Behaviour

Once enough constraints are visible, restate the situation:

> “Given these conditions, this behaviour is a rational response.”

**Pause here.**

This reframing does not absolve individuals of agency or accountability. Nor does it prove that every instance of the behaviour is desirable; it changes the focus from correcting individuals to understanding the conditions within which their choices are being made.

This is often the point at which defensiveness decreases.

The group can now replace:

> “Why won't people do what we need them to do?”

with:

> **“What would need to be different about the system for a different behaviour to become rational?”**

Do not rush immediately from this insight into solution design.

Seeing the system differently is itself a useful outcome.

***

## Step 5: Identify a Constraint Worth Testing

If the group is ready to move beyond sensemaking, ask:

* “Which constraint appears to reinforce this behaviour most strongly?”
* “Which condition makes an alternative particularly costly or risky?”
* “Which condition could plausibly change?”
* “What small change might alter the choices available?”
* “What would we expect to observe if this constraint really matters?”

Focus on changing the **conditions** under which choices are made.

This might mean:

* removing the need for the behaviour
* making an alternative safer
* legitimising something the system currently discourages
* changing when commitment occurs
* changing what evidence governance accepts
* moving or clarifying decision rights

Avoid:

* telling teams to behave differently
* introducing new rules simply to prohibit the behaviour
* creating training as the default intervention
* redesigning the entire system
* assuming the first plausible explanation is correct

A **small** constraint shift is sufficient to learn.

***

## Step 6: Frame Any Intervention as a Hypothesis

If a constraint is selected for adjustment, do not present the intervention as the solution.

State the hypothesis explicitly:

> “If this constraint is materially contributing to the observed behaviour, changing it should alter the behaviour or associated system signals.”

Agree what you would expect to observe. The purpose is not to measure whether people comply with a preferred behaviour. Look instead for evidence that the conditions influencing behaviour have changed.

If the behaviour changes, that provides evidence that the identified constraint was significant.

If it persists, that is also useful information. The map may be incomplete, another constraint may dominate, or the relationship between the constraint and behaviour may have been misunderstood.

Update the map rather than escalating enforcement.

The learning loop is:

**Observe → Map → Hypothesise → Intervene → Observe → Update**

Not every facilitated session needs to complete this loop.

A session may legitimately end after mapping and reframing if the group has not yet developed sufficient understanding to intervene.

***

## Closing the Session

End by capturing:

* what behaviour was actually observed
* what conditions appear to make it rational
* which constraint classes seem significant
* where the inquiry crossed organisational boundaries
* what surprised the group
* what remains uncertain
* which assumptions the group is currently making
* whether any constraint is worth testing

If an intervention has been proposed, capture the hypothesis and the system signals that would provide evidence for or against it.

Do **not** manufacture an action list simply to make the session feel productive.

Insight is a legitimate outcome.

The group may discover that the organisation is unwilling to change the conditions producing the behaviour. That is also useful information.

The organisation can then make an explicit choice: change the constraint, accept the behaviour it produces, or acknowledge the trade-off it is making.

***

## Facilitator Reminders

* Behaviour is information
* Persistence suggests that something may be reinforcing or protecting the behaviour
* Rational does not mean desirable
* Resistance may be rational optimisation
* Silence may indicate perceived risk rather than apathy
* Constraints explain persistent patterns better than intentions alone
* Organisational boundaries do not end the inquiry
* Do not search for a single root cause
* The map is a hypothesis, not truth
* Learning precedes redesign
* Constraint changes are experiments, not fixes

If the conversation becomes personal, return to:

> “What condition makes this response reasonable?”

If the conversation becomes solution-focused too early, return to:

> “What do we still not understand about why this behaviour makes sense?”

***

## EOM Alignment

This facilitation approach directly enacts the EOM principle:

> **To change behaviour, you must change the system that makes that behaviour rational.**

It treats behaviour as evidence about system conditions rather than primarily as something to control.

It looks for **conditions** to understand and potentially change, rather than people to correct or singular causes to remove. It also treats any subsequent system change as a hypothesis subject to learning.

The purpose is therefore not simply to produce different behaviour. It is to understand the system sufficiently well that different behaviour can become rational without enforcement.

***

## Worked example: Deterministic planning & estimation

**Observed behaviour**\
Product teams spend several days each quarter estimating work, sequencing it into a roadmap, and defending delivery dates despite significant uncertainty and repeated divergence between plans and actual delivery

***

### Step 1: Name the behaviour (neutral)

> “Teams estimate epics and stories and construct sequenced quarterly plans before significant discovery or validation has occurred.”

No judgement.

No assumption that planning itself is wrong.

No explanation has yet been attached to the behaviour.

***

### Step 2: Explore Why the Behaviour Makes Sense

The facilitator reframes the inquiry:

> “Rather than asking why teams keep doing this, let's ask:
>
> **What conditions make this the safest or most reasonable option available?”**

The conversation might begin:

> What causes teams to create detailed estimates and sequenced plans before significant discovery has occurred?

The group identifies several possibilities:

* delivery dates are requested before work receives approval
* dependencies need to be communicated to other teams
* portfolio reviews expect plans covering the coming quarter
* funding decisions require an account of what will be delivered

Rather than choosing one explanation, the facilitator expands the inquiry.

> What makes those requirements difficult to satisfy without detailed estimates and plans?

Further conditions emerge:

* governance forums expect dates and milestones
* other teams coordinate their commitments using those dates
* funding decisions happen before substantial discovery
* uncertainty is difficult to represent in existing reporting

The inquiry crosses another boundary.

> Why do those parts of the organisation depend on early commitments?

The group might discover:

* annual budgets require investment decisions before much learning is available
* portfolio governance uses delivery milestones as its primary evidence of progress
* coordination mechanisms assume dependencies can be identified in advance
* leaders are accountable for explaining future delivery to stakeholders

The facilitator continues:

> What happens if a team exposes uncertainty rather than providing a confident estimate?

Possible answers include:

* funding decisions become harder
* portfolio reporting contains information that existing forums do not know how to interpret
* dependent teams cannot construct their own plans
* teams may be perceived as less prepared than teams offering firm dates
* leaders have less certainty to communicate upwards

The inquiry has not uncovered a single root cause.

It has exposed a **reinforcing system**.

***

### Step 3: Surface and Group the Constraints

The group clusters what it has learned.

For example:

> **Funding and investment**
>
> Investment decisions occur before sufficient discovery has taken place.

> **Governance and reporting**
>
> Forward commitments are a recognised form of evidence in portfolio conversations.

> **Coordination**
>
> Other teams depend on dates to construct their own plans.

> **Timing of commitment**
>
> Commitment occurs earlier than meaningful learning.

> **Legitimacy**
>
> Confident predictions may appear more credible than explicit uncertainty.

The group can now see that detailed estimation may serve several purposes simultaneously.

It is not necessary to decide that one of these is the root cause.

***

### Step 4: Reframe the behaviour

The facilitator reflects the emerging map back to the group:

> **“Given that investment, governance and coordination decisions are being made before significant learning is available, and that dates are an accepted way of supporting those decisions, producing detailed estimates and plans is a rational response to the conditions teams face.”**

The insight is more nuanced than:

> “Teams estimate because leadership demands certainty.”

The system has created a set of needs that estimation currently satisfies.

The important question now becomes:

> **“What would need to change for teams to expose uncertainty or learn before commitment without making those other organisational needs impossible to satisfy?”**

Nothing has yet been fixed.

But the behaviour can now be understood in context.

***

### Step 5: Identify a Constraint Worth Testing

The group does not attempt to redesign planning, funding, governance and coordination simultaneously.

Instead, it asks:

* Which condition most strongly reinforces early detailed estimation?
* Which could we change without destabilising everything around it?
* What alternative evidence could satisfy the need currently served by estimates?
* Which decision might safely occur later?
* Where could uncertainty be represented explicitly?
* What would we expect to observe if that constraint changed?

Suppose the group identifies one portfolio review in which detailed delivery commitments are expected before discovery.

A possible constraint shift might be to allow evidence about assumptions, uncertainty and learning to support that decision alongside delivery forecasts.

That is not yet a new organisational process.

It is something that can be tested.

***

### Step 6: Frame the Intervention as a Hypothesis

The group might state:

> **If requiring detailed delivery commitments at this stage materially contributes to premature estimation, then accepting evidence of learning and uncertainty in this review should reduce the need for teams to manufacture detailed certainty before discovery.**

The group then identifies what it would expect to observe.

* Perhaps teams spend less effort producing speculative detail.
* Perhaps uncertainty becomes visible earlier.
* Perhaps investment conversations improve.
* Perhaps nothing changes because another governance forum still demands the same commitments.

All of these outcomes provide information.

If detailed estimation persists unchanged, the group does not conclude that teams have failed to adopt the new behaviour.

It updates its understanding of the constraints.

***

## Outcome

The immediate result of Behaviour → Constraint Mapping is not necessarily behavioural change.

It is improved understanding of the system.

The group may now see that:

* estimation serves organisational needs beyond the team
* apparently irrational behaviour can be locally rational
* multiple constraints reinforce the same behaviour
* changing team practice alone would leave those constraints intact
* uncertainty has consequences elsewhere in the organisation
* any proposed intervention is an hypothesis rather than a guaranteed solution

The organisation can now decide whether to change one of those conditions.

It may also decide that the constraint serves a purpose it is unwilling to relinquish.

Either result is valuable because the trade-off is now explicit.

The facilitation has succeeded when the conversation shifts from:

> “How do we make teams stop doing this?”

to:

> **“What makes this behaviour make sense, and what does that tell us about the system we have designed?”**
